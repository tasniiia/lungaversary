Her story

2012 · The diagnosis

Short of breath

In 2012, my mom went in for a routine doctor’s appointment. Her doctor noticed that just walking across the room made her oxygen levels drop far lower than they should have.

Months of tests and biopsies followed, and with them three diagnoses. Sjögren’s syndrome, an autoimmune disease, had turned her immune system against her own body. Pulmonary fibrosis was slowly scarring her lungs, and pulmonary hypertension was forcing her heart to work harder to pump blood through them. Her lungs simply couldn’t get enough oxygen into her body anymore. Medication could ease her symptoms, but only new lungs could change the ending.

Along the way, she found her people. She joined a number of support groups for others facing the same diagnosis and met many incredible human beings, a few of whom are here tonight.

2013 – 2016 · The wait

The long road to UCLA

Month by month, her condition worsened. Oregon had no lung transplant program, so she went through months of testing to join the list at the University of Washington. But my dad’s work kept him moving from state to state, and with that much uncertainty, we couldn’t plan for a transplant at UW. She stepped off the list.

A year later, sicker than before, she went back. This time UW turned her away, saying she was too high-risk. Her pulmonology team refused to accept that and pointed her to UCLA, where her condition put her straight at the top of the list.

They told her no one would take her.
UCLA did.

2016 · The call

The moment we’d been waiting for

About a month later, the phone rang at four in the morning. Our bags were already packed. My mom flew to Los Angeles by air ambulance, I caught the first flight out, and my dad drove down from Sacramento, where he was working, to meet us there.

2016 · The surgery

The longest night

The surgery ran through the night, and neither of us slept. Our phones never stopped buzzing with calls and texts from everyone who loves her, sending strength, prayers and positivity, asking for updates and offering whatever help they could.

We had no idea whether she would make it out. Spoiler: she did.

The team at UCLA is incredible. They had her up and walking within a day.

2016 – 2017 · The recovery

Learning to breathe again

After three weeks in the ICU, she moved into Tiverton House, UCLA’s hotel for patients and their families. We were supposed to stay three months. She recovered so well that we went home after two.

We were never alone. My mom’s three siblings, two of my cousins, my dad’s brother and other relatives came to see her, and so did a few of my dad’s closest friends. My Nanu, my mom’s mom, even stayed with us for about a month to help with her recovery.

We lost Nanu in 2022, but we will always be grateful that she got to watch her daughter get better. No parent should ever have to endure the loss of their child.

2017 – 2026 · The years since

Getting her life back

As she got stronger, my mom and I explored Los Angeles together, wandering markets and landmarks and Ubering anywhere we could, from the Griffith Observatory to the Dolby Theatre to Chinatown and everywhere in between. She had been handed a second chance, and she wasn’t going to waste a minute of it.

The last ten years

Trips, weddings, friends and family

Once we were home, she said yes to everything: the trips she’d put off for years, the big family moments, and all the ordinary, wonderful days in between.
